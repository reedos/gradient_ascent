Operator craft is the human half of the manual: the skill of working well with a model, which
grows more demanding, not less, as a system climbs the ladder. At level 1 it is mostly one skill,
saying clearly what you want. By level 7 it is several: judging work you did not do yourself,
deciding what to hand over and what to keep, and knowing, from evidence rather than a good first
impression, how much to trust a system that acts without asking each time. This page is the
overview; four pages carry the depth: <Link href="/techniques/briefing/">briefing</Link>,
<Link href="/techniques/reviewing/">reviewing</Link>, <Link
href="/techniques/delegating/">delegating</Link>, and <Link
href="/techniques/trust/">calibrating trust</Link>.

None of this is specific to a coder. A domain professional who has never written a line of code
briefs, reviews, delegates and trusts a model exactly the way a builder does, on the same four
skills, just without the code.

here reports a measured number.

<Lanes>
<Lane name="use" title="Use it">

Briefing is the skill everything else rests on, and it is mostly the discipline of not assuming
the model already knows what you mean. Anthropic's own prompting guidance states the underlying
point plainly: "Claude responds well to clear, explicit instructions"<Cite n={1}/>, and the <Link
href="/techniques/briefing/">briefing</Link> page quotes the fuller version of that guidance and
takes it apart into six parts. What you want, the constraints it has to respect, and what "done"
looks like are three separate things. Writing them on three lines, not in one paragraph, is the
whole discipline: a missing constraint is invisible in prose and obvious in a list.

How much to spell out depends on which model you are briefing, and OpenAI's own guidance sorts
its models into two kinds for exactly that reason<Cite n={2}/>. The <Link
href="/techniques/briefing/">briefing</Link> page quotes that passage and works an example
through it; the overview's job is only to say that the newcomer's mistake runs in both
directions: over-specifying a model that could have worked out the details, or under-specifying
one that needed them spelled out.

Reviewing work you did not do is a different skill from doing the work yourself, and it gets
harder, not easier, as a system does more without you. Anthropic's own guidance for agent
builders describes agents pausing "for human feedback at checkpoints or when encountering
blockers" as a normal part of the design, not a fallback, and lists coding as a case where "Code
solutions are verifiable through automated tests"<Cite n={3}/>. Even there it says human review
remains crucial, the sentence <Link href="/techniques/reviewing/">reviewing</Link> quotes in full.
The practical version: check what you can verify independently (a citation that resolves, a number
you can recompute) before trusting the parts you cannot. The failure mode that follows has a
name and a literature: a scale-development paper in Frontiers in
Psychology summarizes the agreed definition of complacency as a human operator monitoring an
automated system, at a frequency that is "suboptimal or below a normative rate," where that
suboptimal monitoring "leads to performance failures"<Cite n={4}/>. The trust was real; it just
stopped being checked against evidence.

</Lane>
<Lane name="build" title="Build it">

A builder shapes operator craft through the interface, whether or not any of this is written
down as a rule. A free-text chat box makes the brief and the request the same blob of prose, so
nothing stops the constraint or the definition of done from getting silently dropped on a rewrite.
A task input with separate fields (what to do, what not to do, what finished looks like) costs
a little more to build and makes the brief a thing the reader can check against later, not just a
paragraph they typed and moved past.

Reviewing needs something to review. A system that shows only a final answer gives a reviewer
nothing to check but the answer's surface plausibility; one that shows its sources, and ideally
the steps that produced the answer, lets a reviewer check the parts that can be verified
independently, the way this site's own trace player exposes each step of a run instead of only
its last one. Anthropic's guidance for agent builders treats the interface between a model and
its tools as real design work rather than plumbing: one rule of thumb it gives is to think about
how much effort goes into human-computer interfaces and "plan to invest just as much effort in
creating good agent-computer interfaces"<Cite n={3}/>. The surface a reviewer reads deserves the
same budget.

Approval points are where operator craft becomes a piece of code, not just a habit. An interface
that lets a person approve or reject one specific action before it runs (rather than trusting an
instruction in a system prompt to hold) is the same shape as the permission check on the <Link
href="/techniques/safety/">safety, privacy and governance</Link> page: a check outside the model,
that runs whether or not the model would have gotten it right on its own. A builder designing for
delegation decides, ahead of time and in code, which actions get that checkpoint and which do
not; leaving that decision to be made informally, in the moment, is how it stops getting made at
all once volume goes up.

No runnable example accompanies this page. A task-input form, a review surface, and an approval
checkpoint are interface and process decisions, not an algorithm with a fixed answer to test
against: the honest Build it lane here is what to design for, not code to run.

</Lane>
</Lanes>

<WhenNot>

Skip building any of this out formally (a task-input form with separate fields, a visible review
surface, a coded approval checkpoint) for a single, low-stakes request you would not mind redoing
by hand. Writing the constraint and the "done" condition on their own line, the way the Practices
below ask, is already most of the value, and it costs nothing to try before building anything
around it.

There is nothing to brief, review, delegate or trust before a model is actually in the loop;
<Link href="/techniques/order-zero/">level 0, no model at all</Link> does not raise any of these
questions.

Build the formal version once a wrong answer costs enough, or gets reviewed by someone other than
the person who asked, that "I would have caught it" stops being a good enough plan.

</WhenNot>

## At each level

- <Link href="/levels/0/">No model</Link>: there is nothing to brief, review, delegate or trust
  yet; this topic's skill only starts once
  <Link href="/techniques/order-zero/">a model is actually in the loop</Link>.
- <Link href="/levels/1/">One call</Link>: briefing is nearly the whole skill, since a single
  <Link href="/techniques/prompt-engineering/">prompt</Link> is the entire interface between what
  you want and what you get back.
- <Link href="/levels/2/">Context</Link>: reviewing has to include what the model was given, not
  only what it said: <Link href="/techniques/rag/">RAG</Link>'s own failure modes show a wrong
  answer built from the right sources is a different problem than one built from missing ones,
  and telling the two apart is its own skill.
- <Link href="/levels/3/">Workflows</Link>: a fixed pipeline hands you a defined checkpoint, the
  way <Link href="/techniques/human-in-the-loop/">human approval</Link>'s own pause-and-resume
  shape does, so reviewing can happen at a point along the way instead of only at the end.
- <Link href="/levels/4/">Tools</Link>: delegating a specific action, not just an answer, becomes
  a real decision (<Link href="/techniques/function-calling/">function calling</Link> is exactly
  that decision in code), and trusting a model to describe what it would do is not the same judgment
  as trusting it to actually do it.
- <Link href="/levels/5/">Agents</Link>: the model chooses its own steps, so calibrating trust
  (how much to check, and how often, instead of checking every single step
  <Link href="/techniques/single-agent/">a single agent</Link> takes) replaces reviewing each one
  by hand.
- <Link href="/levels/6/">Teams of agents</Link>: reviewing shifts toward reviewing a division of
  labor, not one output: whether <Link href="/techniques/orchestrator-workers/">a lead's
  split</Link> of the task was sensible is a separate question from whether each part came back
  right.
- <Link href="/levels/7/">Always-on agents</Link>: delegating reaches its hardest form: deciding
  in advance what an agent may do without asking and what it must always ask about, the three-way
  policy <Link href="/techniques/agent-teammates/">always-on assistants</Link>' own example
  builds, since there is no longer a moment where a person is watching in real time to decide.

## Practices

- Write the constraint and the "done" condition down separately from the request itself, not
  folded into one paragraph that's easy to reread without noticing a piece went missing.
- When reviewing work you didn't do, check what you can verify independently first (a citation
  that resolves, a number that recomputes) before trusting the parts you can't check that way.
- Decide what to hand over by what a wrong answer costs and how fast you'd notice it: the same
  question <Link href="/techniques/order-zero/">level 0, no model at all</Link> asks about choosing a level at
  all, applied here to one task instead.
- Calibrate trust from a running record of corrections over time, not from how confident the most
  recent answer sounded.
- Treat a string of correct answers as a reason to keep sample-checking, not a reason to stop:
  that is exactly where the research on automation complacency says monitoring erodes.
