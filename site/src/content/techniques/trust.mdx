Calibrating trust means keeping how much you rely on a model without checking in line with how
often it has actually been right on tasks like the one in front of you. It is a record, not an
impression, and it is built one checked result at a time: which is why
<Link href="/techniques/reviewing/">reviewing</Link> comes first: the reviews are the entries.
It is the last of the four skills on the <Link href="/techniques/operator-craft/">operator
craft</Link> topic, and what the record is *for* is the next round of
<Link href="/techniques/delegating/">delegating</Link>.

Both directions cost something. Over-trust looks exactly like things going well, right up until
the wrong answer that mattered goes through unread. Under-trust looks like diligence: re-doing
work a model has done reliably a hundred times, or avoiding a task it would genuinely help with
because a different task went badly once. Neither shows up as an error anywhere.

The unit of trust is a task type, not a model. Reliably right at summarizing a document you can
check says nothing about arithmetic, or about a question outside anything it was trained on. A
single global "I trust this one" hides which specific things it has earned.

<Lane name="use" title="Use it">

A record for one person is five columns and takes about twenty seconds a row. Keep it wherever
you already keep notes:

| Date | Task type | What you checked | Right? | What you changed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 12 Mar | renewal dates from a contract | all 4 dates against the clauses | yes | nothing |
| 12 Mar | plain-language summary of a policy | the 2 exclusions it listed | no | added the third exclusion |

The columns that do the work are the second and the fifth. **Task type** is what makes the record
usable later: "renewal dates from a contract" and "tone of a client reply" are separate lines
even if the same product did both. **What you changed** is the only column that says how wrong it
was: nothing, a word, or the whole answer. A month of that tells you, per task type, how often
you are actually editing rather than accepting, and that number is your sampling rate for the
next month.

Two rules keep the record honest. Log the checks you *did*, including the ones that came back
fine; a record of corrections alone reads as a catastrophe. And re-check the record whenever what
you are trusting changes: a new model version, an edited prompt, a new tool. Anthropic states
this for its own published techniques: where one names a specific model, "treat it as measured on
that model and re-check it against your own evals before applying it to another."<Cite n={1}/> A
record built on last quarter's version does not transfer just because the product name did.

It is worth being careful about what the research here establishes. A 2019 scale for automation
complacency was built from a survey of 475 online-panel participants whose automation experience
was mostly low-stakes. Its authors summarize prior work as expecting trust and complacency to go
together, then report that their own monitoring subscale overlapped far less with a general
propensity to trust machines than their other subscale did<Cite n={2}/>. Being a trusting person,
in that data, did not predict whether you kept watching. Those participants were not supervising
models, so this is not a finding about your work, but it is a reason to keep a count per task
rather than trusting a disposition you think you have.

A 2025 position paper surveying over-reliance research defines the risk as "relying on LLMs
beyond their capabilities" and locates it across "diverse domains from healthcare to personal
advice"<Cite n={3}/>. It argues for measurement rather than reporting a measurement: which is
the same conclusion as the table above, from a much larger literature.

<Lane name="build" title="Build it">

A team cannot keep one person's notebook. What replaces it is a fixed set of checkable questions,
scored the same way every time and kept as a file: the <Link href="/techniques/evals/">evals</Link>
topic pointed at a running system rather than at a prompt being drafted. "We have been using it a
while and it seems fine" is the thing the file exists to replace.

Three properties make a team record worth keeping:

- **Broken out by task kind**, the way this site's own eval set splits its questions, so a good
  score on easy lookups cannot stand in for the record on the cases nobody re-checked.
- **Versioned by what produced it**: model id, prompt version, tool set. A rise or a fall is
  only informative if you can name what changed; without that, the record is a mood.
- **Fed automatically where it can be.** A result that carries a citation, the way
  <Link href="/techniques/rag/">RAG</Link>'s does, can have the mechanical part checked by
  machine: the reviewing page's own checker reports whether a figure the answer states appears in
  the section it cites. That is one row of evidence per answer without a person reading the whole
  thing, and it is a presence check, so it is a floor under the record, not the record.

Sample by hand on top of that, at a rate you write down. The machine check and the human sample
answer different questions, and an automated number rising while nobody has read an output in
six weeks is exactly the state that looks safest and is not.

One design decision belongs here rather than in the record: what the system does when its own
confidence is low. A system that can say "I did not find this" gives a reviewer a signal worth
logging; one that always produces an answer makes every result look identical from outside, and
a record over identical-looking results is much more expensive to keep.

## At each level

- <Link href="/levels/0/">No model</Link>: nothing to calibrate: a rule passes its tests or it
  does not, and it behaves the same way tomorrow.
- <Link href="/levels/1/">One call</Link>: every reply is independent, so the record is simply a
  tally per task type with nothing else to attribute a change to.
- <Link href="/levels/2/">Context</Link>: the record has to separate "found the right source and
  read it wrong" from "never found it", because those two have different fixes.
- <Link href="/levels/3/">Workflows</Link>: the steps are fixed, so trust can be tracked per
  step, and one unreliable stage stops dragging down the ones around it.
- <Link href="/levels/4/">Tools</Link>: the record now needs to cover the action taken, not only
  the sentence produced: a wrong call can be reported in perfectly correct prose.
- <Link href="/levels/5/">Agents</Link>: the unit becomes a whole run of variable length, so the
  record tracks outcomes and cost per run rather than accuracy per answer.
- <Link href="/levels/6/">Teams of agents</Link>: agreement between agents is not evidence. The
  record has to cover the division of labour, since a team can be confidently wrong together.
- <Link href="/levels/7/">Always-on agents</Link>: the record is the only thing standing in for
  someone watching, so it has to be written by the system itself and read by a person on a
  schedule.

## Practices

- Keep the record per task type. "It has been good lately" is not a record.
- Log the checks that came back fine as well as the ones that did not; otherwise the record only
  contains disasters and reads like one.
- Set the sampling rate from the last month's edit rate, and write the number down where someone
  else can see it.
- Re-check after any change to the model, the prompt or the tools, and mark the record with what
  changed.
- Name the tasks you deliberately do not trust, and what evidence would change that. An untested
  assumption in the cautious direction is still an untested assumption.
