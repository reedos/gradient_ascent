"How do you know it worked" has a different answer at every level, and the answers are not
replacements for each other. A schema check does not tell you an answer is true. A second model's
verdict does not tell you the system got better this month. A score on a fixed set does not tell
you which step produced a bad answer yesterday. Each of the five pages here answers one of those
questions, and a system that has climbed a few levels usually needs several of them at once.

The order below is the order the checks appear as you climb, not an order of importance.

## Five checks, and the question each one answers

| Level | Page | The check | What it cannot tell you |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | <Link href="/techniques/structured-output/">Structured output</Link> | the reply has the fields your code expects, and your own side validates and retries once | whether the values are right: a validly typed number can still be wrong |
| 3 | <Link href="/techniques/evaluator-optimizer/">Write and check</Link> | a second prompt tests the draft against criteria written in advance, and the loop repeats until it passes | anything the criteria do not state; a checker asked whether an answer is "good" just drafts again with extra steps |
| 6 | <Link href="/techniques/debate-review/">Review and debate</Link> | a separate agent, with its own context and often its own retrieval, decides what to check and whether to accept | whether the reviewer is right: it is another model call, and it costs a run of its own |
| Topic | <Link href="/techniques/evals/">Evals</Link> | a fixed set with known answers, run the same way before and after a change | which step caused any individual failure |
| Topic | <Link href="/techniques/observability/">Observability</Link> | a record of what each step actually did: the passages retrieved, the tool called, the branch taken, the tokens and the time | whether the result was right, which is what the other four are for |

## What changes as you climb

**The check moves from the shape of the answer to its content.** At level 1 the thing you can
check cheaply is the shape. Structured output's page is direct about the limit: a schema
constrains the form, not the facts, and a reply can still fail to match at all, on a refusal or a
response cut short by the token limit. The validate-and-retry-once pattern exists because of that
gap, not in spite of it.

**A criterion has to be checkable to be worth anything.** Write and check is the first level where
a check runs inside the system, and the phrase its page leans on is "clear evaluation criteria".
A specific, checkable question, such as whether every citation appears in the sources it names,
answers the same way every time. A vague one drifts between calls, which is a checker that costs a
call and settles nothing.

**Independence is what level 6 buys, and it is not free.** The difference between write and check
at level 3 and review and debate at level 6 is not that one is stricter. At level 3 the loop and
the criterion are both your code's: a fixed loop, one narrow test written in advance. At level 6
the reviewer is itself an agent deciding what to check and whether to accept, with its own
retrieval, and your code's job shrinks to running that loop and those searches faithfully. You
pay for an agent to get a reviewer that can notice something your criteria did not anticipate.

**A score is about a change, not about an answer.** Evals is the only one of the five that
compares two runs rather than judging one, which is why the site treats it as a topic rather than
a level: a single prompt, a fixed workflow and an agent that runs for hours all make claims only
a fixed set can check. It also comes with its own soft spot, stated on its page: a rubric grader
is itself a model call and can be wrong, so some share of its verdicts needs a person's reading.

**You cannot check what you did not record.** Observability is last here because it is what makes
the other four investigable after the fact. A score that fell tells you something changed; a trace
tells you which step. What is worth recording changes by level, which is why that page keeps its
own list per level rather than one for the whole site.

## Reading it as one argument

Each check is cheapest at the level it appears, and each is a poor substitute for the next one
along. A schema is free and shallow. A checker prompt costs one call and can only test what you
wrote down. An independent reviewer costs a run and can look for what you did not think of. An
eval set costs the work of building it once and is the only thing that can say a change helped. A
trace costs storage and answers "where", never "whether".

None of that is an argument for having all five. It is an argument for knowing which question you
are actually asking before you pay for an answer to a different one.
