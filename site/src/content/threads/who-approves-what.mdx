The usual way to picture the climb is that a person does less at every level, until at the top
they do nothing. That is not what the pages here describe. A person is present at every level of
this site, including the last one. What changes is the thing they hold: at first the answer
itself, then each action before it happens, then the rules a whole run happens inside, then only
the exceptions a run decided to raise.

Reading these six pages in order follows that one decision as it moves. None of it is new here:
each page below makes its own case with its own sources, and this thread only puts them in the
order the question actually travels in.

## Before anything is handed over

<Link href="/techniques/delegating/">Deciding what to hand over</Link> comes before any of the
levels, because it is a question about the task rather than about the model. Its four questions
are what a wrong answer would cost, how easily the result could be checked, how reversible the
action is, and whether the task needs context only you have. A system perfectly able to draft
something can still be the wrong thing to let send it.

That page belongs at the front of this thread for a practical reason: every design below is an
answer to it. A gate at level 3 and an allowlist at level 5 are the same decision written into
different parts of the system.

## What moves, and what does not

Three things change across that table.

**The unit gets bigger.** At level 3 a person sees one draft. At level 5 they do not see the
individual steps at all; they set what the loop may do and read the log afterward. The harness
page is blunt about how much of an agent is not the model: the loop, the tool definitions, what
goes into the next request, whether an action needs approval, the caps, the sandbox and the
logging all sit outside it, and changing any one of them changes how the same model behaves.

**The moment moves earlier.** Approval at level 3 happens after the draft exists. An allowlist
happens before the run starts. The later the level, the more of the reviewing is done in advance,
as design, and the less of it is done in the moment, as reading.

**The trigger stops being a person.** At level 7 a schedule or an event starts the session, which
is ordinary code, and then the model decides whether anything needs attention and what to do
about it. That is the decision that actually moved, and it is worth separating from the timer: a
cron job that runs fixed steps is a level-3 workflow whatever time it fires at.

One thing does not change. Somebody still has to be able to tell whether the result was right,
and <Link href="/techniques/reviewing/">reviewing work you did not do</Link> gets harder rather
than easier as more of the work happens where nobody was watching. Two conditions have to hold
for a result to be reviewable at all: there has to be something to check it against, and enough
of the work has to be visible to check rather than just its conclusion. A design that removes
both has not reduced the reviewing, it has removed the possibility of it.

## Where a reader usually goes wrong

Treating the gate as the safety feature. The gate is a place to put a decision; the rule that
decides when it fires is what does the work. Human approval's own page says both ways of getting
the threshold wrong cost something: too loose and it waves through what most needed a second
look, too strict and approving becomes a reflex.

Reading the top of the climb as unattended. An always-on assistant's design question is what it
may do without asking, which is a question with an answer, not an absence of one.

Trusting a model rather than a task. Calibrating trust makes the point directly: the unit is a
task type, not a model, and reliably right at one thing says nothing about another. A single
global judgment hides which specific things have actually been earned.
