Before an SRB-5030 board goes on the bench, someone has to answer one question: how high can
the input go. The number lives in the datasheet's Recommended Operating Conditions table,
section 3: 36.0 V. It also lives in ECN-2608-04, an engineering change notice that lowers it to
32.0 V for board revisions A and B and leaves 36.0 V standing only for revision C, a board that
has not shipped yet. The datasheet itself has not been reissued to match. An engineer who opens
the datasheet, the document everyone reaches for first, and stops at the number in the table
gets 36.0 V, which is wrong for every revision A or B board actually in the building.

This is what an engineer actually has: not one authoritative datasheet, but a stack of documents
that update each other. `evals/bench/corpus/` holds the whole stack for the SRB-5030: the
datasheet, the test specification, four instrument programming manuals, a bill of materials,
design-review rules, a bring-up notebook, a failure-analysis guide, a calibration procedure, and
the one change notice, 12 files and 78 numbered sections in total. Answering a question like
this means finding the right section or sections and saying plainly which board revision the
answer is for, with something a person can go check. Nothing here decides whether a board
passes: <Link href="/recipes/limits-without-a-model/">limits without a model</Link> is where a
measurement meets a limit. This recipe only answers what the documents say, which is a question
that comes up long before, and quite apart from, any single test.

## The lowest level that answers it, and why not higher

Level 0 here is a keyword search over the same 12 documents, BM25 over the section text, with a
person reading the results. For the question below, that search puts the datasheet's Recommended
Operating Conditions section first and both of the change notice's relevant sections second and
third, of 78. The datasheet section a person reads first even states the problem outright:
"The 36.0 V maximum in this table is superseded for revision A and revision B boards. See
ecn-2608-04.md." A reader who reads that sentence, not just the number in the table above it, is
most of the way to the right answer with no model anywhere.

<WhenNot>Try <Link href="/techniques/order-zero/">order zero</Link> keyword search first if a
person is going to read the top few hits themselves. It already ranks the datasheet's
Recommended Operating Conditions section and the change notice inside the top three of 78
sections for the question this page walks through.</WhenNot>

So the case for level 2 here is not that retrieval is hard: it plainly is not. It is that a
person chasing one footnote at a time does not scale to a shop asking dozens of these questions
a day, and a narrower lookup, "find the max input voltage in the datasheet," can return the
datasheet's number and never reach the footnote at all. <Link
href="/techniques/rag/">RAG</Link>, retrieving broadly across the whole corpus rather than one
document, and <Link href="/techniques/structured-output/">structured output</Link>, requiring
the reply to name which revision it is for and rejecting a citation list that drops the notice
once the notice was retrieved, do that chasing in code every time, instead of depending on
whoever happens to be reading that day.

Climbing to <Link href="/techniques/agentic-rag/">agentic RAG</Link> (level 5), where the model
runs a second search depending on what the first one found, buys nothing extra here. That is
worth saying plainly, because the job shape this page belongs to says the opposite: documents
disagreeing about a number that depends on a revision is normally a reason to climb. Three
things make this corpus the exception. The two conflicting numbers use enough of the same words,
"maximum," "input voltage," the revision letters, that one search returns both the datasheet
section and the notice: 1st and 6th of the top 8 with the hashing stand-in embedder this repo
tests against, and 1st and 3rd of 78 with BM25 and no embedder at all. The corpus is 78 sections,
so a top-8 cut is a tenth of everything. And the notice names what it supersedes, the document in
its own header and the section in its first paragraph, which is what lets code check a citation
list instead of hoping.

Change any of those three and the argument goes with it. A corpus of thousands of sections, where
the cut that fits in a prompt is a fraction of a percent, can leave the notice out of the
candidate set with nothing to say so. A notice that does not name what it supersedes gives the
check below nothing to key on, and finding it at all means reading the first answer and searching
again on what it said, which is the dependent second search level 5 exists for. A notice
superseded by a later notice makes that chain longer than one hop. Any of those, and the extra
calls and the extra ways to be wrong start buying something. On this corpus they do not.

## The walkthrough

No example on this site has called a live model yet (`docs/EVALS.md`), so nothing below is a
recorded trace: it is worked by hand from the same code and the same stub the tests exercise,
and is labeled illustrated because it is one.

The question: "What is the maximum input voltage of the SRB-5030, revision B, per its
recommended operating conditions?" `run()` loads all 78 sections of `evals/bench/corpus/`,
embeds the question and every section, and keeps the top 8 by cosine similarity. Two of those
eight are the ones that matter: `ecn-2608-04#1` ("Change," the notice itself, ranked 1st of 8)
and `srb5030-datasheet#3` ("Recommended Operating Conditions," ranked 6th of 8). The other six
share some vocabulary but not the conflict: a calibration procedure, two instrument manuals, and
two more sections of the datasheet and the notice. All eight go into one prompt, with a JSON
schema requiring an `answer`, an `applies_to_revision`, and a `citations` list.

A correctly scoped reply looks like this:

```
{
  "answer": "The maximum input voltage is 32.0 V, not the datasheet's 36.0 V.",
  "applies_to_revision": "A and B",
  "citations": ["ecn-2608-04#1", "srb5030-datasheet#3"]
}
```

Code checks three things before this is handed back as the answer: every citation was actually
one of the eight retrieved sections, `applies_to_revision` is not blank, and, because
`srb5030-datasheet#3` is cited, `ecn-2608-04#1` is cited too, since the notice was retrieved and
does supersede that section. A reply missing any of those gets one retry with the specific
problem appended to the prompt; a reply still wrong after that comes back labeled unvalidated
rather than returned as an answer.

## What it costs

One question through this example, scripted with the correctly scoped reply above, costs one
model call: 1,977 tokens in (the eight retrieved sections plus the schema and the question,
counted by `count_tokens`) and 42 tokens out, both pinned by a test so this page cannot drift
from the code. A reply that fails validation costs a second call at roughly the same size, since
the retry only appends the validation error. Level 0 costs nothing: a keyword search runs in
milliseconds over already-loaded text, with no call to anything.

An engineer does not ask this once per board. They ask it when a fixture is set up for a
revision, when someone new joins the line, or when a question like it comes up in a design
review. A shift where it gets asked twenty times over is on the order of 40,000 tokens in and
840 out. That is small next to re-embedding all 78 sections, which this example does on every
single call; caching the corpus's embeddings, which do not change between questions, is the
obvious next optimization and is not done here.

## How to evaluate it

<HowToEval>

This example is not one of the ones `evals/questions.json` and `scripts/eval_run.py` score (see
`docs/EVALS.md`): that set is graded against `evals/corpus/`, the Halvorsen appliance documents,
and this recipe answers questions about a different corpus with a different, revision-sensitive
answer shape. A bench-specific eval would need its own small golden set, a dozen questions to
start, over `evals/bench/corpus/`, each with a known right value, a known right
`applies_to_revision`, and the exact citations a right answer has to include.

A right answer here is not just the correct number. It is the correct number, the correct
revision, and, wherever a retrieved change notice supersedes what another retrieved passage
says, a citation to that notice. Grading stays code, the way it is everywhere else on this
site: compare the parsed value and revision against the golden answer, and compare the citation
set against a `must_cite` list, the same citation hit rate <Link
href="/recipes/document-qa/">document Q&A</Link>'s eval section already tracks. The asymmetry
worth watching is not accuracy overall, it is a reply that gets the number right and stays
silent on the revision: that is the one shape of wrong answer this recipe exists to catch, and a
golden set with no revision-sensitive question in it would never measure it.

</HowToEval>

## How to adapt it

What ports: chunk by a corpus's own numbered sections rather than writing a splitter, retrieve
broadly enough that a conflicting document is actually in the candidate set before code can
check for it, and require a reply to name what a value applies to rather than accepting a bare
number. `SUPERSEDED_BY` in `_validate` is the specific move worth keeping: whenever a newer,
retrieved document supersedes something an older one says, a reply citing the older one without
the newer one does not validate.

What does not: the corpus loader's assumption that every document is Markdown with numbered `##
N. Title` headings (a real datasheet is usually a PDF, with tables that need their own parser),
the `SUPERSEDED_BY` map itself, which is hand knowledge about this one bench and not something
code discovered on its own, and every number here: the SRB-5030's name, its 36.0 V and 32.0 V
figures, ECN-2608-04's own number. A reader's own documents carry their own supersession
history, to read and hand-encode the same way, or to replace this one small dictionary with a
general mechanism, which this example does not attempt.

The same shape shows up anywhere a newer document quietly changes what an older one says without
the older one being reissued: an amendment against a contract, an errata sheet against a
standard, a policy update against a handbook, one instrument's calibration procedure revised
without its programming manual catching up. The reasoning carries over even where nothing is
electronics: retrieve widely enough to see both documents in one pass, and never let an answer
name a value without saying which version of the document it came from.
